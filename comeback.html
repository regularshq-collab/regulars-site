— The whole argument

Winning a brand new customer means competing on ads, on the map, on price, against everyone else in town. Getting one back means reminding somebody who already knows your work that you exist.

Most owner-operators have years of these names sitting in a phone, a notebook, or a job-management app nobody exports. That list is the most valuable thing in the business and it is doing nothing.

03 / Evidence

The maths on
keeping people

Retention is one of the oldest findings in business research, and the number that matters most to you came from an auto-service chain.

Harvard Business Review

The paper that started this whole field asked a simple question: what happens to profit when a business just loses fewer customers?

An auto repair chain that held on to 5% more of its customers saw profits rise 30%. An insurance brokerage saw 50%. A bank's branches, 85%.

The auto repair number is the one worth sitting with. That is a trade business with vehicles, technicians and a customer list — not a software company.

Reichheld & Sasser, “Zero Defections: Quality Comes to Services”, Harvard Business Review, 1990. Read it

Journal of Marketing Research

Researchers studying win-back made a plain point: almost every business spends its money either chasing new customers or holding on to current ones. Almost nobody spends anything on the people who already left.

Which is odd, because those people already know your work and already chose to pay you once. They are the cheapest names on the whole list to reach.

That is the gap Comeback sits in. Not new customers, not current ones. The ones you already won and quietly stopped talking to.

Thomas, Blattberg & Fox, “Recapturing Lost Customers”, Journal of Marketing Research, 2004. Read it

You will see “a 5% lift in retention raises profit 25–95%” quoted all over the internet. That range is later marketing, not the study. The figures above are the ones actually in the 1990 paper, and none of them are results Regulars has produced.
